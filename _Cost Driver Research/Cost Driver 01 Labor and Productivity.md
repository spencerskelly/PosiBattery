# Cost Driver 01: Labor and Productivity

**Scope:** Warehouses, distribution centers, and production facilities using material-handling equipment (MHE)  
**Research date:** October 2026

## Executive findings

Labor-productivity products reduce cost by removing travel, eliminating manual transaction steps, improving task allocation, automating repetitive transport, shortening training, and preventing rework. The right product depends on whether the dominant loss is walking, waiting, poor scheduling, manual data capture, material movement, training, or unavailable equipment.

The major commercial categories used today are:

- Warehouse, execution, and labor-management systems.
- Voice-directed work and wearable scanning.
- Pick-to-light and put-to-light.
- Collaborative warehouse autonomous mobile robots (AMRs).
- Goods-to-person and automated storage/retrieval systems (AS/RS).
- Manufacturing AMRs and autonomous lift trucks.
- Connected-worker platforms.
- Forklift telematics and connected-power orchestration.

Published productivity figures are site-specific and often come from vendor-sponsored case studies. They demonstrate achievable outcomes, but should not be used as guaranteed forecasts without a controlled site pilot.

## Cost mechanisms

| Productivity loss | Operational need | Product response | Costs reduced |
|---|---|---|---|
| Walking and empty travel | Reduce non-value-added movement | AMRs, goods-to-person, optimized pick paths | Direct labor, overtime, fatigue, peak staffing |
| Searching and waiting | Make assets, work, and exceptions visible | RTLS, telematics, readiness dashboards | Paid idle time, supervision, missed throughput |
| Paper and manual entry | Digitally guide and validate work | Voice, wearables, lights, connected-worker apps | Transaction labor, errors, paper |
| Poor work prioritization | Assign the right task and resource | WMS, WES, LMS, orchestration software | Idle time, overtime, late shipments |
| Repetitive transport | Automate predictable moves | AMRs, autonomous forklifts, conveyors | Driver and indirect material-handling labor |
| Skill and training gaps | Guide execution and preserve knowledge | Digital instructions, voice, skills management | Onboarding, trainer time, early-tenure errors |
| Errors and rework | Validate item, location, quantity, and sequence | Scanning, voice confirmation, lights, vision | Rework, returns, shortages, expediting |
| Process imbalance | Expose task time, queues, and utilization | LMS, WES, analytics, digital time studies | Overstaffing, underutilization, line starvation |
| Equipment unavailability | Predict readiness and trigger action | Telematics, battery analytics, CMMS integration | Waiting, rentals, overtime, schedule recovery |

## WMS, WES, and LMS

### Products and functions

Blue Yonder provides warehouse and labor-management functions for labor planning, standards, intra-shift reallocation, performance reporting, incentives, and visibility into tasks and downtime.[cite:179][cite:181] Manhattan Active Warehouse Management combines inventory, labor, automation, yard, task assignment, and pick-path optimization on a shared platform.[cite:191]

### Needs solved

- Manual allocation of people and equipment.
- Uneven workloads between zones and shifts.
- Excess overtime caused by late visibility.
- Limited understanding of true task difficulty and travel.
- Inconsistent coaching and labor-performance measurement.

### Reported business outcomes

- Blue Yonder reports that DSC Logistics achieved more than a 20% reduction in variable labor spend across its network.[cite:179][cite:186]
- Henkel reports improvements in speed, accuracy, efficiency, service, and cost control using Blue Yonder warehouse and labor-management products, although its public case does not quantify savings.[cite:180]
- Manhattan reports that Simon & Schuster increased variable labor productivity by more than 10% and reduced overtime by more than 20%.[cite:182]
- Manhattan materials report a 25% labor-productivity gain at Vera Bradley from automation, system-directed work, and warehouse management.[cite:184]

### Costs reduced

- Regular labor and overtime.
- Temporary staffing and emergency schedule changes.
- Supervisor planning and reporting.
- Late-order recovery and service failures.
- Unrecognized idle and indirect time.

### Limits

WMS/LMS products optimize work but do not physically remove travel or handling. Their effectiveness depends on clean location, inventory, task, equipment, and labor data. Engineered standards and individual monitoring also require validated methods, worker communication, and appropriate labor-policy governance.

## Voice-directed work

### Products and functions

Honeywell Voice directs mobile workflows with hands-free, eyes-free instructions.[cite:238] Lucas Jennifer combines voice, speech recognition, scanning, displays, RFID, batching, pick-path optimization, and coordination of workers and robots.[cite:224][cite:225][cite:227]

### Needs solved

- Repeatedly reading paper or handheld screens.
- Keyboard entry and manual check-off.
- Hands-busy picking, loading, replenishment, inspection, or kitting.
- Long onboarding for location-intensive work.
- Poor batching and inefficient pick paths.

### Reported business outcomes

- Honeywell reports that Al Nahdi Medical achieved picking accuracy above 99.5% and double-digit worker-productivity improvement.[cite:154][cite:159]
- Würth Austria reported approximately 15% higher picking performance, pick errors reduced from 3 to 2 per thousand, and elimination of about 500,000 paper sheets annually.[cite:163]
- Simco reported 10–15% higher productivity, more than 60% fewer picking errors, and approximately 1,000 fewer reams of paper annually after deploying Lucas voice-directed picking and loading.[cite:153]
- Lucas reports a 36% average picking-productivity gain across customers, but actual results vary by process and baseline.[cite:149][cite:156]

### Costs reduced

- Picking, replenishment, and loading labor.
- Overtime and peak headcount.
- Training and shadowing.
- Errors, returns, and reshipments.
- Paper, printers, and administration.

### Limits

Voice is strongest in repetitive processes where workers need both hands. Complex visual decisions may require a display or scanner. Acoustic conditions, language support, headset hygiene, Wi-Fi, user acceptance, and WMS integration must be validated.

## Wearable computing

### Products and functions

Zebra’s WT6400 is an Android wearable computer for warehouse, distribution, and manufacturing workflows. It supports wearable scanners, voice accessories, an integrated camera, and Wi-Fi 6E.[cite:226][cite:229][cite:232]

### Needs solved

- Picking up and putting down handheld terminals.
- Occupied hands while handling product or containers.
- Slow barcode capture and screen navigation.
- Device-related ergonomic and handling interruptions.

### Reported business outcomes

Signet reported a 40% reduction in picking time, approximately 8.3 hours saved per operator per week, and $67,000 in annual operational savings after deploying Zebra wearables.[cite:162]

### Costs reduced

- Direct transaction labor.
- Scan and selection errors.
- Device-handling time.
- Some device damage and ergonomic interruption.

### Limits

Wearables provide an execution interface, not warehouse optimization by themselves. Benefits depend on the application, workflow, WMS logic, network, and scan strategy. Fit, weight, battery changes, freezer operation, gloves, cleaning, and scanner range require user testing.

## Pick-to-light

### Products and functions

Dematic supplies pick-to-light and put-directed systems using lights, quantity displays, confirmation buttons, scanners, conveyors, and execution software.[cite:210][cite:212]

### Needs solved

- Slow picking from compact high-velocity locations.
- Worker dependence on memorized locations or printed lists.
- High training burden for repetitive processes.
- Wrong-item and wrong-quantity picks.

### Reported business outcomes

- Dematic reports designed pick rates of 160–300 pieces per operator-hour in its Claire’s implementation, with improvements in accuracy, productivity, and order-processing time.[cite:210]
- UNILAB deployed pick-to-light, mobile terminals, zone routing, and control software to reduce waiting and travel, reporting improved productivity and peak-handling capacity without a published percentage.[cite:216]
- Dematic reports 99.9% overall order accuracy at Magid, where pick-to-light supports a goods-to-person system.[cite:220]

### Costs reduced

- Picking and put-wall labor.
- Training and supervision.
- Error correction and reshipment.
- Late-order recovery.

### Limits

Light systems fit dense, stable, repetitive pick faces. Value falls with frequent SKU relocation or distributed work. Hardware installation and reconfiguration are more involved than software-only or voice solutions.

## Collaborative warehouse AMRs

### Products and functions

Locus Robotics supplies collaborative AMRs and orchestration for picking, putaway, transport, and fulfillment workflows. The fundamental mechanism is transferring walking and cart transport from people to robots.[cite:165][cite:175][cite:177]

### Needs solved

- Excessive person-to-goods walking.
- Cart pushing and movement between zones.
- Peak labor and temporary staffing.
- Long training for facility navigation.
- Inflexibility of fixed conveyor systems.

### Reported business outcomes

- DHL reported more than 150 units per hour versus a 78-UPH baseline, more than twice the order volume with the same headcount, and 50% shorter cycle time in a life-sciences site.[cite:174]
- APL Logistics reported a 122% increase in picking productivity and training reduced from days to hours.[cite:164][cite:165]
- Staples Canada nearly doubled productivity from 42 to 82 units per hour and reduced average shift duration from 13 hours to 8.[cite:168]
- Locus customer materials also report 61% productivity improvement and 20% lower labor needs at Motivational Fulfillment, 300% higher picking productivity at Dental City, and 111% higher UPH at Fleet Feet.[cite:166][cite:171][cite:175]

### Costs reduced

- Direct picking labor.
- Overtime and temporary labor.
- Walking and order-transport time.
- Training and some ergonomic/turnover costs.
- Fixed automation changes in variable operations.

### Limits

AMRs require WMS integration, traffic management, charging, wireless coverage, safety validation, exception processes, and sustained utilization. Results must be normalized for SKU velocity, order profile, travel baseline, and robot utilization.

## Goods-to-person and AS/RS

### Products and functions

AutoStore uses a dense grid, bins, robots, ports, and software to deliver inventory to operators. Dematic Multishuttle similarly automates storage/retrieval and presents totes at goods-to-person workstations.[cite:167][cite:220]

### Needs solved

- High walking and searching across large assortments.
- Expensive floor space or building expansion.
- High labor for access, replenishment, and picking.
- Need for controlled inventory access and ergonomic stations.

### Reported business outcomes

- AutoStore’s customer materials report nearly 50% lower labor cost and 50,000 square feet reclaimed at Northern Tool; 400% higher pick productivity and 30% more storage capacity at Radwell; and 150% higher throughput with labor needs cut in half at Outdoor Network.[cite:167][cite:170]
- Dematic reports that Magid’s system supports about 1,300 orders and 2,600 cartons per day, 99.9% order accuracy, and 98.5% same-day shipment while eliminating operator search for inventory at the workstation.[cite:220]

### Costs reduced

- Picking and replenishment labor.
- Overtime and peak staffing.
- Occupancy and expansion.
- Errors, loss, and cycle counting.
- Some ergonomic and injury-related costs.

### Limits

Goods-to-person systems require substantial capital, engineering, integration, commissioning, maintenance, and contingency planning. Product dimensions, fire protection, structural loading, redundancy, manual fallback, and downstream balance must be validated.

## Manufacturing AMRs

### Products and functions

Seegrid provides autonomous tow tractors and lift vehicles for parts-to-line, staging, pallet movement, and finished-goods transport.[cite:217][cite:218]

### Needs solved

- Repetitive point-to-point driving.
- Production workers leaving stations for material.
- Inconsistent milk runs and line-side delivery.
- Labor shortages in indirect handling.
- Desire to avoid fixed conveyors.

### Reported business outcomes

- Whirlpool has used Seegrid AMRs for more than a decade to move parts and finished products in an Ohio facility producing more than 20,000 washing machines daily; an independent award report states that the deployment reduces labor dependency and supports productivity goals.[cite:211]
- Guardian Industries reports improved productivity, lower variability, safer labor allocation, and rapid ROI, although the public account does not quantify savings.[cite:223]
- Sumitomo automated a 3,000-foot round trip performed 30–40 times per day and reported a three-day startup.[cite:219]
- A vendor-described Seegrid workflow released more than 10 full-time equivalents of capacity and projected more than $1.5 million in five-year savings; this requires site-specific validation.[cite:213]

### Costs reduced

- Tugger and forklift driving labor.
- Overtime for repetitive transport.
- Line-starvation and schedule recovery.
- Work-in-process buffers in controlled deployments.
- Some safety and product-damage exposure.

### Limits

Feasibility depends on payload, fork interfaces, traffic, pickup/drop consistency, doors, congestion, charging, empty-container logic, and production integration. Automating an unstable route can preserve waste instead of removing it.

## Connected-worker platforms

### Products and functions

Poka combines digital work instructions, training, skills, forms, communication, and analytics.[cite:194][cite:198] Augmentir provides AI-supported instructions, skills management, and workflow integration.[cite:196][cite:197] Tulip provides composable frontline applications for production, quality, inspection, and workflow automation.[cite:203][cite:205]

### Needs solved

- Searching for current instructions or expert help.
- Process variation across shifts and facilities.
- Long onboarding and shadowing.
- Loss of tribal knowledge.
- Paper forms and delayed production data.
- Poor visibility into worker qualifications.

### Reported business outcomes

- Bosch reported an 8% direct-productivity gain and 54% improvement in mean time between repair during a Poka pilot.[cite:194][cite:204]
- Augmentir reports that a battery manufacturer improved productivity by 17% and reduced onboarding time by 40%.[cite:196][cite:197][cite:200]
- Augmentir reports another implementation with more than 1,060 workflows across 10 plants, 10–30 minutes saved per shift, and up to 120 minutes removed between maintenance notification and work-order closure.[cite:196]
- Tulip customer materials report 50% higher testing volume at Test Devices, 60% less inspection/rework time at TICO Tractors, and a 30% faster clinical-packaging process at Sharp Packaging.[cite:203]
- A Forrester study commissioned by Tulip modeled 15% direct-labor efficiency improvement and 50% indirect-labor time savings for a composite customer. This is a modeled composite, not a guaranteed deployment result.[cite:205]

### Costs reduced

- Training, shadowing, and refresher labor.
- Assembly, inspection, and maintenance time.
- Engineering, supervision, and reporting effort.
- Rework, scrap, and investigation.
- Downtime from delayed troubleshooting.

### Limits

These platforms require content ownership, revision control, approvals, multilingual support, device strategy, and retirement of old instructions. Digitizing a poor method does not improve it. Value rises when workflow data integrate with MES, ERP, CMMS, quality, and skills systems.

## MHE telematics and connected power

### Products and functions

Commercial products such as Raymond iWAREHOUSE and Powerfleet On-Site combine utilization, impacts, operator access, electronic checklists, maintenance, and battery visibility.[cite:101][cite:102][cite:103][cite:112]

### Needs solved

- Time spent finding a usable truck, battery, or charger.
- Paper inspections and manual compliance records.
- Poor knowledge of moving, loaded, idle, and unavailable time.
- Oversized fleets caused by weak utilization data.
- Unexpected truck, battery, or charger unavailability.

### Costs reduced

- Driver idle and search time.
- Overtime caused by unavailable equipment.
- Supervisor and administrative labor.
- Rentals, spare assets, and premature fleet additions.
- Maintenance and downtime.

### Product adjacency

A connected battery/charger platform can convert energy and readiness data into labor-productivity outcomes through:

1. **Truck–battery–charger identity:** Maintain association history by shift and location.
2. **Shift-readiness prediction:** Forecast whether available energy supports planned work.
3. **Charger assignment:** Direct operators to a compatible, available charger.
4. **Queue management:** Detect and prevent charging congestion.
5. **Utilization inference:** Estimate working, idle, charging, and unavailable states from battery signals.
6. **CMMS workflows:** Generate and close maintenance work automatically.
7. **Operator instructions:** Replace generic alerts with explicit next actions.
8. **WMS/LMS integration:** Correlate equipment state with tasks, moves, and paid time.
9. **Indoor location:** Add BLE, UWB, or Wi-Fi options for asset finding.
10. **Cost-per-productive-hour:** Combine waiting, energy, maintenance, battery depreciation, and truck cost.

The strategic role should be equipment readiness and energy workflow, integrated with WMS, LMS, CMMS, telematics, and automation products rather than replacing them.

## ROI measurement

### Baseline data

Collect at least four representative weeks, including peak conditions where practical:

- Paid direct and indirect hours by process and shift.
- Units, lines, cases, pallets, moves, or completed operations.
- Travel, waiting, handling, transaction, and exception time.
- Overtime and temporary labor.
- Training hours and time to standard performance.
- Errors, rework, returns, and recovery labor.
- Equipment availability, utilization, search, and queue time.
- Throughput, backlog, cutoff misses, and production-starvation events.

### Core calculations

- **Labor productivity:** completed work units / paid labor hours.
- **Unit labor cost:** fully burdened labor cost / completed good units.
- **Released capacity:** baseline hours at post-deployment volume minus actual hours.
- **Realized labor saving:** eliminated overtime, temporary labor, vacancies not backfilled, or reassigned labor that produces measured output.
- **Annual net value:** labor saving + capacity value + error/rework saving + avoided capital minus annual operating cost.

Released hours are not automatically cash savings. The business case should distinguish payroll reduction, avoided hiring, overtime elimination, and redeployment to incremental output.

### Pilot controls

- Compare equivalent products, order profiles, routes, and shifts.
- Normalize for volume, mix, seasonality, learning, and staffing changes.
- Track adoption and workaround rates.
- Separate product impact from slotting, layout, and policy changes.
- Reconcile product data with payroll, WMS/MES, and finance records.
- Re-measure after launch effects and intensive coaching end.

## Product strategy

For a connected battery/charger business, the strongest labor-productivity position is an **equipment-readiness and workflow layer**.

### Build

- Common truck–battery–charger asset identity.
- Remaining-runtime and shift-readiness models.
- Charger assignment and queue management.
- Automated maintenance and exception workflows.
- Cost per productive truck-hour and energy per move.
- APIs/webhooks for WMS, LMS, CMMS, telematics, and BI.
- Cross-vendor battery and charger normalization.

### Partner or integrate

- WMS/WES/LMS for task and labor context.
- Voice and wearable suppliers for operator interaction.
- RTLS suppliers for precise location.
- Forklift OEMs and telematics providers for direct vehicle state.
- AMR/AS/RS providers for automation status and energy readiness.
- Connected-worker platforms for maintenance and inspection procedures.

### Extended features

- Truck/battery edge gateway for BLE, UWB, or RFID.
- Operator identification and qualification checks.
- Electronic pre-use inspection.
- Motion, impact, and load sensing.
- Local display or mobile assignment application.
- Demand-aware charger power management.
- Prebuilt WMS and CMMS workflow connectors.
- Cross-site benchmarking by shift, vehicle class, and chemistry.

## Risks and conflicts

- Vendor case studies use different baselines and definitions; results are not directly comparable.
- Released labor capacity may increase throughput without reducing payroll.
- Productivity incentives can encourage unsafe shortcuts unless quality and safety guardrails are included.
- Individual monitoring can create privacy and workforce concerns.
- Automation may move labor into replenishment, maintenance, packing, or exceptions rather than eliminate it.
- Gains can be constrained by downstream bottlenecks.
- Cloud products need offline and degraded-mode behavior.
- Safety-critical controls require formal hazard analysis and local protection.
- Mixed fleets increase integration and lifecycle-support complexity.
- Poor master data can undermine every optimization layer.
