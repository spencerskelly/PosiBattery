# Forklift Clean Fuel Credit Business Case

## Definition

Business case note on whether clean fuel credits for forklift charging justify charger-side data and metering features: credit prices, credit yield per MWh, market evidence and the gaps that block a decision. Evidence sits on the Document notes; disagreements are numbered C296 onward in [[Battery Product Landscape Conflicts and Open Questions]].

## Notes

- **Reading rule:** sources are second-hand through a summarizing fetch tool; trade, vendor and utility sources are T4 and interested parties; agency sources sit outside T1 to T5. Calculations are the analyst's and are labeled. Status as of 2026-10-17.
- **Question:** do clean fuel credits give forklift owners enough money to justify charger-side data and metering features? This note gathers prices, per-MWh yields and market evidence, and lists what is missing. It does not recommend a product investment.

### Bottom line (analyst summary)

1. **California credit value is about $33 to $63 per MWh of charging energy** at the $72.61 per tonne mid-2026 average, depending on the forklift class: $33.29 for trucks under 12,000 lb (EER 2.4) and $62.62 for 12,000 lb and over (EER 3.8), using the 2026 benchmark 80.17 and grid CI 65.07 (analyst calculation from the proposed regulation text, [[Document - CARB LCFS Second 15-Day Changes Attachment A-1 Proposed Regulation Order (after 2024-08-12)]]). The earlier $25 to $68 range used a stale benchmark and is kept below as superseded (C304). The adopted order is still unread (C296).
2. **Charger efficiency changes the reportable energy by up to 11.8 percent** when the meter is upstream of the charger (95 percent versus the 85 percent default), worth about $3.33 per MWh metered at EER 2.4 (analyst calculation). Guidance 25-01 also requires the meter to be within 5 percent accuracy ([[Document - CARB LCFS Guidance 25-01 Forklift Fueling Reporting (2025-10)]]).
3. **No source gives annual kWh per forklift,** so revenue per truck cannot be computed from sources. The one worked example found (PG&E, $1,360 a year) cannot be reconciled with CARB's current factors (C297). Real kWh data from PosiLink or E-Meter would close this (IB-258). Oregon's approved 2023 estimation method caps estimated use at 30 percent depth of discharge and two shifts a day ([[Document - Oregon DEQ Electric Forklift Estimation Methodology Memo (2023-06-09)]]); that is an estimation ceiling, not a measured consumption figure.
4. **Prices differ a lot by program and are mostly stale:** California $72.61 in mid-2026 (CARB), Washington $45.92 average for 2024 (Ecology), Oregon $47.98 in April 2025 (newsletter) but $80.51 in DEQ's 2025 cost report ([[Document - Oregon DEQ 2025 Annual Costs and Benefits of the Clean Fuels Program]]; C301), and a vendor claim of Canadian credits above $400 each in early 2026. Oregon and Washington 2026 prices were not obtained (the Oregon Q1 2026 summary read has no price, [[Document - Oregon DEQ Clean Fuels Program First Quarter 2026 Data Summary]]).
5. **Forklifts are a material share of credits where counted:** Oregon had more than 3,500 registered electric forklifts in May 2023, producing over a third of electricity credits ([[Document - Oregon DEQ Forklift Methodology Meeting Deck (2023-05-30)]]).

### Calculation: California credits per MWh of charging energy (analyst calculation, corrected 2026-10-17)

Credits (tonnes) = (benchmark x EER - grid CI) x 3.6 MJ per kWh x 1,000 kWh per MWh / 1,000,000. Inputs: benchmark **80.17 gCO2e/MJ** for 2026 and EER 2.4 (under 12,000 lb) and 3.8 (12,000 lb and over) from the proposed regulation order in [[Document - CARB LCFS Second 15-Day Changes Attachment A-1 Proposed Regulation Order (after 2024-08-12)]] (the adopted order is unread, C296, C298); grid CI 65.07 gCO2e/MJ for 2026 ([[Document - CARB 2026 California Average Grid Electricity Carbon Intensity Update]]); price $72.61 per tonne ([[Document - CARB Weekly LCFS Credit Transfer Activity Reports Page (retrieved 2026-10-17)]]). It assumes forklift charging uses the annual-average grid CI.

| Forklift class (EER) | Credits per MWh (t) | Value per MWh at $72.61 | Value per MWh per $10 of price |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12,000 lb and over (3.8) | 0.863 | 62.62 | 8.63 |
| Under 12,000 lb (2.4) | 0.458 | 33.29 | 4.58 |
| 1.9 (an earlier proposal named in a 2024 comment letter; not found in CARB text) | 0.314 | 22.81 | 3.14 |

Reporting a heavier truck's energy under the under-12,000 lb class (which Guidance 25-01 requires when classes cannot be told apart) loses about 47 percent of that energy's credit value.

### Illustration: per-truck order of magnitude (hypothetical inputs, not sourced)

Oregon's 2023 estimation formula ([[Document - Oregon DEQ Electric Forklift Estimation Methodology Memo (2023-06-09)]]) gives kWh per charge = depth of discharge (max 30 percent) x battery kWh x charger efficiency x charge return factor. With a hypothetical 30 kWh battery, 92 percent efficiency, 115 percent charge return, two shifts and 250 days: 9.5 kWh per charge and about 4,760 kWh a year. At EER 2.4 and $72.61 that is about $158 a year per truck (4.76 MWh x $33.29). Real trucks usually discharge deeper than 30 percent (analyst reasoning), so this is a low case. All inputs are assumptions; real kWh per truck is still the missing number (IB-258).

### Oregon reference point (regulator-computed)

DEQ's 2025 calculation values electricity at $0.0858 per kWh, or $85.80 per MWh, from a standard of 88.25 gCO2e/MJ, an electricity CI of 3.91 gCO2e/MJ, an EER of 3.4 and a $80.51 credit price ([[Document - Oregon DEQ 2025 Annual Costs and Benefits of the Clean Fuels Program]]). It is a program-wide electricity figure, not a forklift one. The very low CI (against 65.07 in California) is why Oregon electricity is worth more per MWh at similar EER; whether forklift charging uses it was not found (IB-264).

### Market evidence found

- **California:** CARB counts more than 89,000 spark-ignited forklifts statewide across all fleets (see [[California Forklift Electrification Regulations Register]]); the electric share is unknown.
- **Oregon:** more than 3,500 electric forklifts registered by May 2023; forklift credits over a third of electricity credits [[Document - Oregon DEQ Forklift Methodology Meeting Deck (2023-05-30)]].
- **Vendors already sell the data layer:** Smart Charging Technologies sells meters at the charger output, panel or forklift ([[Document - Smart Charging Technologies Letter to Oregon DEQ on Forklift Metering Options (2023-05-15)]], T4); 3Degrees and 7Gen sell credit-generation services ([[Document - 3Degrees Revenue Streams from Electric Forklifts Under Clean Fuel Standards (2025-09-25)]], [[Document - 7Gen CFR Credit Price in 2026 (2026-06-11)]], T4). A charger maker would compete with, or partner with, these.
- **Utility role:** PG&E tells fleets they may generate credits and offers help with a dedicated meter ([[Document - PG&E EV Fleet Low Carbon Fuel Standard Flyer (undated)]]).

### Gaps that block a business decision

- **Annual kWh per forklift** and the share of customers that claim credits: needs PosiCharge fleet data and customer interviews (IB-258, IB-252).
- **Adopted California forklift EER** and the 2026 benchmark in force: the proposed text gives 2.4, 3.8 and 80.17; the adopted order is unread (C296, C298; IB-242).
- **Oregon and Washington 2026 prices and EER values** (IB-259); Oregon forklift EER and CI (IB-264).
- **Verification cost:** verification of forklift electricity is required from the 2026 data year (statements due 31 August 2027); small reporters (under 6,000 credits) may defer up to two years or give up the credits ([[Document - CARB LCFS Guidance 25-01 Forklift Fueling Reporting (2025-10)]]); cost per site not found (IB-265).
- **Aggregator fees:** what vendors keep from each credit (IB-262).
- **Competing and partner offers** at the charger or panel level (IB-263).

### Implications (analyst reasoning, hypotheses only)

- **H11:** the credit is worth tens of dollars per MWh in California at current prices. For a truck charged with 10 MWh a year (an illustrative figure, not sourced), that is a few hundred dollars a year; the actual figure depends on the real kWh. Whether this changes a purchase decision depends on fleet size and on how much customers value avoiding manual meter work. This is not a demonstrated willingness to pay.
- **H14:** the 2026 California price gap between truck classes (about 2x per MWh) makes the lift-capacity class the largest single lever in the data layer; the guidance's lowest-class default penalizes any site that cannot tag charging by truck (REQ-00061, REQ-00063).
- **H12:** the most robust product value is accurate, auditable energy data (identity of the measuring point, no hidden adjustment), because it serves California, Oregon and the New Mexico metered-kWh rule at once (REQ-00058).
- **H13:** efficiency disclosure is worth a few dollars per MWh in California; this is small compared with the data and verification effort, so it is a secondary lever.
